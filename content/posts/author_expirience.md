There are plenty of online guides that explain a step-by-step guide to self 
publication, but not many of them truly explain what it is like to actually write one; 
the fun parts, the difficult times and everything in between.  <br>
I hope to provide a vague sense of this journey and what to look forward to (for
better or for worse) while doing it.

## The Planning

If you have an idea, <i>don't rush the process.</i> <br>
The single best thing that I (unintentionally) did in this entire project was `planning`. In fact,
I would go as far as saying that <i>I reckon it wouldn't have finished</i> if I didn't do one. <br><br>

With me, the idea for my story was dreamt one random morning. I sprang up as soon as I woke and hopped onto my computer to write 
the main concepts for the novel; just a few of the major scenes. Over the next few days, I kept designing scattered worldbuilding 
elements such as the charecter names, backstories and main places of interest. <br><br>

{{< callout type="tip" >}}
Saying the cruciality of planning, it's worth mentioning that you shouldn't spend forever planning out every individividual scene.
The writing <a href="#"> (which I will get to) </a>  was spent joining together these few ideas that I had, and that made the whole 
experience more enjoyable.
{{< /callout >}}

Put as simply as possible, thinking ahead of your current timeline is inevitable and was even expected in my case--formatting, 
cover design, publishing, or possibly further than that--but you just have to `embrace it` and move on. <br>
Always remember to <i>focus on the main task at hand,</i> because nothing matters more than the present, and 
overcomplicating it with steps you aren't even up to won't do you any good.
After this document stretched about ten pages, I began to write.


## The Writing

When I began, I had no intentions for it to actually complete it. The only initial thing planned was the first chapter; to properally
get my idea onto the page. But from there, <i> I never looked back. </i> <br><br>

The most enjoyable part of the enire process linking; brainstorming new ideas to bond the charecters, develop their personalities
or take a few <i>off the board</i>. I'd say 80% of my scenes were thought of while writing, and those that wern't were modified in variation 
in order to fit the context from the previous story. <br> <br>

The most challenging was the pressure. The constant feeling that, if you wern't writing, you were spending your time unproductively.
I didn't have any time constraints at the begining, which allowed me to work on it without any stress as a mere side project. 
But as time went by, I decided to link it with project at school. Although giving me a inscentive to finish sooner, the deadline also 
made it quite stressful near the end. <br> <br>

Each page correlated to <i>at least</i> fourty minutes of work; <b>before <a href="#">editing</a><b>. Sometimes this could be lots more; 
mainly on unplanned scenes with casual charecter building. It didn't help that my perfectionism made me dwell over small wording changes
or phrases that just "didn't sound right". Some days were difficult to get going, but I always felt great after writing.

## Editing

After finishing writing the main story
I saught to do this untraditionally, with no outside assistance. 
You would be suprised how many mistakes you will find. If you do plan to seek proffesional assistance, I would strongly advise you to
look over the manuscript by yourself, so the editor will do a better job
This is one of the few processes that help would have likely been appriciated.
Actually, a heap of the editing was done <i> after </i> I the book was published.
